How does your phone know where you are?

GPS satellites broadcast their position and time. Your phone uses these signals to calculate where you are. A clock that is wrong by a millionth of a second can add nearly 300 metres to a distance measurement. Find out why, then try calculating a location yourself.

Why timing matters

Light travels about 30 centimetres in a nanosecond. A delay of just one microsecond adds nearly 300 metres to a measured range. Range error and position error are not necessarily equal.

How the algorithm works

Each simulated satellite broadcasts a known position. We calculate a true geometric distance using the Euclidean distance formula, divide it by the speed of light to obtain travel time, then add timing offsets. The estimator receives only satellite coordinates and measured ranges, not the true receiver position.

For one signal the possible positions form a circle. For two signals we calculate circle intersections. For three or more, we minimise the sum of squared range residuals using an iterative Gauss–Newton solver. In the advanced lab we optionally add a shared range bias as another unknown.

Model limits: this is a scaled, flat 2D simulation with illustrative satellite positions, not an orbital model or a real navigation service. Actual GPS uses 3D pseudoranges, satellite ephemerides, clock corrections and atmospheric models.

Part 4 · Advanced experiment

Real GPS: three dimensions and a clock

Satellites send their time and orbital information, from which the receiver obtains their positions. The receiver measures a pseudorange (an apparent distance), not a perfect distance. There are four unknowns: x, y, z and the receiver clock offset. Four independent measurements are normally needed; extra satellites help estimate a solution when measurements disagree.

How the receiver solves four unknowns

For satellite i, the model is ρᵢ = √((x − xᵢ)² + (y − yᵢ)² + (z − zᵢ)²) + b, where b = c × clock offset. Each measured pseudorange gives one equation. The code starts with a position guess and predicts all ranges. It calculates the differences between predicted and measured ranges, then adjusts all four unknowns together using the derivatives of the range equations. This repeats until the adjustment is tiny or the cost stops improving.

With more than four signals, the equations can disagree. Least squares chooses a position and clock offset that minimise the sum of squared residuals. A small residual does not always mean an accurate position: a biased measurement can move the answer while still fitting the data.

The experiment uses fixed illustrative satellite positions at roughly GPS orbital distances above a local receiver coordinate system. It is not an actual satellite orbit or a latitude/longitude navigation solution.

Part 5 · Apply what you learned

Why GPS gets confused in cities

A signal can bounce off a building before it reaches your phone. The reflected route is longer than the direct route. If the receiver mistakes that reflection for the direct signal, it overestimates the distance. Try to recover the position of a traveller by excluding the reflected measurement.

How the reflection is calculated

We mirror the receiver across the wall and draw a straight line from the satellite to that mirrored point. Where the line crosses the wall is the reflection point. The total length of the two real segments is the reflected path. The additional travel time equals the extra path length divided by the speed of light. This measured range is passed into the same least-squares estimator as the other signals.

Real receivers use more sophisticated multipath mitigation. Here we assume a single ideal reflection from a vertical wall, with a receiver using that reflected signal instead of the direct one. Satellite positions are scaled to a small 2D map for clarity.
